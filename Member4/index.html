What LunaWise answers

A commercial lander at the lunar south pole needs sunlight for power and a line of sight to Earth for radio. The Sun skims the horizon there, so a ridge tens of kilometres away decides whether a site is lit, and Earth bobs low enough to slip behind hills for days. When a launch slips, the whole pattern shifts. LunaWise shows which sites still meet your requirements, which fail, when and why.

How it is computed

  • Sun and Earth positions: every hour of 2027, from NASA NAIF SPICE kernels in the MOON_ME frame, cross-checked against JPL Horizons.
  • Terrain horizon: 720 directions from each site, out to 200 km, over LOLA 5 m site maps and the 20 m polar map, with the Moon's curvature included. Mast height 2 m.
  • Visible means the Sun's or Earth's disc centre is above the terrain horizon in its direction (point Sun/Earth).
  • Mission rules (window, slip, first failure, battery) run in your browser from those hourly flags, in one shared module.
  • Ray-cast check: a separate ray-march over the heightmaps re-tests every hour in the background. The 3D views show how often it agrees with the engine.

Checked against NASA Goddard

NASA Goddard PGDA product 69: 18.6-year average at the surface, 60 m pixel. Ours: 2027, hourly, at the surface (0 m) to compare like with like; mission results use the 2 m mast. Difference in percentage points.

Likely reasons for differences (likely, not proven): the 60 m pixel averages lower ground around these high points; a different time span; a different observer height; Goddard counts Earth visible if any part of its disc clears the terrain, we use the disc centre.

Limits, stated plainly

  • Most 5 m terrain pixels are interpolated between laser tracks (about 90%, Barker et al. 2021).
  • Point Sun and Earth, 2 m mast, one-hour steps, 2027 only.
  • "Earth visible" is a clear geometric line of sight, not a radio link budget.
  • The 3D lander view draws terrain out to about 30 km; farther ridges are in the horizon line but not drawn as mountains.
  • The battery is a scenario assumption, not an official NASA spacecraft specification.
  • The whole-Moon photo map is a low-resolution mosaic for context only; the south-pole relief is the team's LOLA heightmaps.

Sources
